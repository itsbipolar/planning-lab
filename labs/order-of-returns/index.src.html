The Order of Returns

Same average return. A different retirement. Two fictional retirees, the same ten market years in a different order, and what that order does once money starts coming out.

demo. Synthetic people and synthetic market years: not historical, not a forecast.

Meet Ruth and Sam

Ruth and Sam are made up. Each retires with and takes out in the first year, a little more each year after to keep up with prices.

Over their first ten years, their savings see exactly the same ten market years. Only the order differs.

    The average of the ten is a year, for both of them. After those ten years, every year earns that same average.

    Ruth gets the good years first. Sam gets the bad years first.

    Shuffle the years

    First, imagine neither of them takes any money out. Shuffle the order of the years as often as you like.

    With nothing coming out, the order cannot matter: each year multiplies the savings, and multiplying in a different order gives the same answer.

    Now take money out

    Now both take out the same amount each year. Bad years early hit Sam while his savings are largest, and every withdrawal in a down year locks in the loss: that money is gone before the good years come.

      Try to rescue Sam's plan

      Sam's order cannot change. These are things planners look at with people in his position. None of them is right for everyone, and none of them is a recommendation.

      Each lever has a cost: spending less is a smaller life in those years, and cash held aside earns nothing in this model.

      Questions worth asking

      Nothing here can say what anyone should do. It can help with the questions to take to a qualified professional who knows the whole picture:

      • Which of my costs are fixed, and which could bend in a bad year?
      • What would a poor first few years mean for my plan, and how would I want to respond?
      • How much do I want to hold outside the market, and what does that cost me?
      • Which steady income (workplace pensions, CPP, OAS) covers the costs that cannot bend?
      • How do taxes and fees change these numbers for me?
      • What if I live much longer than I expect?

      What this leaves out

      Taxes, fees, real market history (these ten years are invented), spending that changes with age, pensions and government benefits, and every year being different from the average after year ten. It is a picture of one mechanism, not a plan.
